A macro that stopped itself and an 800 kg squat

Seven stories: an Esc press that stopped everything, an 800 kg squat, a minus-800 target, a backup that never happened, a sideways photo and vanished stocks.

21 November 2026 · BergBuilds

Pi: the macro that pressed Esc

Pi is an auto clicker for Windows that runs whole sequences of clicks and key presses. Instead of new features, this round asked one question: what breaks the features it already has?

The best answer was a recorded Esc. Esc is Pi's emergency stop, and Windows hands the keys a program presses to the hotkeys too. So a sequence that pressed Esc to close a popup stopped itself, and the popup never saw the key. Now the hotkey steps aside for that one press. The same round found that pausing in the middle of a held Ctrl left Ctrl held down, so your own typing turned into shortcuts. A pause now lets go of every held key and presses it again when you resume.

Take away: if your tool can press keys, it can press its own off switch. Test your features against each other, not just one at a time.

Reppr: an 800 kg squat

Type 800 instead of 80 in a workout log, and until this week that 800 kg squat stayed forever: a record nobody could beat, and a coach cheerfully suggesting 805 next time. The only way out was deleting the whole workout.

Now the tick asks first whether 800 kg was a typo, shows your heaviest squat so far and offers 80 with one tap. Past workouts also got a button to fix their sets, and if you fill in every set but forget to tick them, finishing asks whether to log those too instead of quietly dropping them.

Take away: people will type nonsense by accident. Compare a new number with their own history, and make the fix one tap instead of a lecture.

Nibblr: the Sunday that asked for minus 800

Nibblr treats the week as one budget: eat a bit less on Monday and you get a bit more later. Fresh eyes on the code found the dark side of that idea. After a big pizza night on Saturday, Sunday's week-adjusted target could drop below zero. No app should tell anyone to eat negative calories.

Now the target never goes more than a quarter under the day's goal, and the app says so. The rest of the overshoot is simply let go. The same review lowered the protein suggestion for heavier users: 2 g per kilo is 260 g a day at 130 kg, so protein now goes by the weight that fits your height.

Take away: any number your app works out from other numbers needs a floor and a ceiling. Try the extremes before your users do.

Greed: the backtest that graded itself

Greed tests stock strategies and runs the best ones on pretend money. After a chip sell-off the momentum account was 42% below its peak, and the dashboard called its gap to the S&P 500 almost never seen: the lowest 0.03%.

Then the catch. The history it compared against ran right up to today, so the live weeks were partly being compared with themselves. And it started in 2012, skipping the 2008-09 crash that momentum is famous for. Fixed, the honest line reads: a fall this deep happened once before, in 2008-09, and took over five years to win back. A fairer yardstick helped too: a real momentum fund fell 22% over the same days, so much of the gap was the bet on only 20 stocks.

Take away: when you ask “how unusual is this?”, make sure the answer can't include the thing you're asking about. And this is an experiment with pretend money, not investment advice.

Nibblr: the backup that never happened

Nibblr keeps everything on the phone, so it keeps an eye on how long it's been since you saved a backup file. On Android you save it through the share sheet, and closing the share sheet looks exactly like saving the file. So the app thought you'd backed up and went quiet for a month, while nothing had been saved anywhere.

Now it simply asks whether you saved it. The same bug hunt found a label scanner that could read the kJ number as kcal, which makes a food look about four times as rich as it is, and a home-screen widget that only spoke English. All three are fixed in a test build, waiting for a real phone to try them on.

Take away: when the system can't tell you whether something worked, ask the user instead of guessing in your own favour.

Zeta: the photo lying on its side

Zeta is the video editor that cuts BergBuilds' devlogs from a script. This afternoon it was fed what a new user actually has, starting with a portrait photo from a phone. Phones often store those sideways, with a small note in the file that says “turn me 90 degrees”. ffmpeg reads the note, so the finished video looked fine, and that hid the bug. Everything else, the part that ranks shots, the face finder that aims the crop and the thumbnails, saw the photo lying on its side. One test picture with the note set proved it, and now every part sees it upright.

The same round moved captions on vertical videos in from the edges, out from under TikTok's like and share buttons, and turned “Error opening input files: End of file” into a message that names the half-copied file and says what to do.

Take away: when one part of your pipeline quietly fixes the input, the other parts may still see it broken. Test with the files real people have, not the ones you made.

Greed: the stocks that vanished from the past

Every backtest has a ghost problem. Companies that went bust or were bought out disappear from free price data, so the past looks like it was full of survivors, and a strategy tested on it looks better than it was. Instead of guessing how much, Greed measured it against a real fund that held every one of those companies: the survivors-only version came out about a percentage point a year ahead since 2012. That's the size of the flattery.

Then the twist: the leak was still happening. The free data source keeps only the last price of a stock that's gone, and every nightly refresh replaced the saved copy, so two delisted companies lost twenty years of history in seven weeks. Now the saved history never forgets.

Take away: free data is written by the winners. Measure the bias against someone who held the losers. And this is a paper-trading experiment, not investment advice.

What's out and what isn't

Lofye is the one BergBuilds app on Google Play right now. Reppr, Nibblr and Arcmode are on their way to a closed test there, and the app fixes in this post are in test builds that aren't out yet. Pi, Zeta and Greed are tools built for use here, not something you can download.
